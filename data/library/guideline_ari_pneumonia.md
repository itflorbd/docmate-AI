# Clinical Practice Guideline: Management of Community-Acquired Pneumonia in Adults
**Source Title**: WHO & National Clinical Guidelines for Adult Acute Respiratory Infections
**Publication Year**: 2024
**Evidence Level**: Ia
**License**: Open Access / Clinical Demo Material

---

### Section 1: Clinical Presentation & Diagnostic Criteria
Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) in adults is defined as an acute infection of the pulmonary parenchyma acquired outside a hospital setting. Classical symptoms include cough, fever, pleuritic chest pain, dyspnea, and production of purulent or rust-colored sputum. On physical examination, tachypnea (respiratory rate > 20 breaths/min), tachycardia, and localized inspiratory crackles or bronchial breathing with dullness to percussion over the affected lung segment are characteristic findings. In elderly patients, atypical presentations such as new-onset confusion or acute functional decline without high fever are frequently observed.

### Section 2: Severity Triage & CRB-65 Score
Every suspected patient must be risk-stratified using the CRB-65 criteria (Confusion, Respiratory rate ≥ 30 breaths/min, Blood pressure < 90 mmHg systolic or ≤ 60 mmHg diastolic, Age ≥ 65 years). A score of 0 denotes mild pneumonia suitable for outpatient management. A score of 1 to 2 denotes intermediate risk requiring hospital assessment or close outpatient observation. A score of 3 or 4 indicates severe pneumonia requiring immediate hospital admission, continuous pulse oximetry, and urgent intensive care consultation.

### Section 3: First-Line Empirical Antimicrobial Regimens
For previously healthy adult outpatients with mild community-acquired pneumonia without comorbidities or known hypersensitivity, oral Amoxicillin 500 mg to 1000 mg three times daily (every 8 hours) for 5 to 7 days is the recommended first-line therapy. Clinical improvement is expected within 48 to 72 hours. In regions with elevated atypical pathogen prevalence or when Legionella or Mycoplasma is suspected, Azithromycin 500 mg orally on day 1 followed by 250 mg once daily on days 2 to 5, or Clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily, may be added or used as first-line monotherapy.

### Section 4: Alternative Regimens & Penicillin Hypersensitivity
In patients with documented non-severe penicillin allergy (e.g. mild rash), Cefuroxime axetil 500 mg twice daily for 7 days or Cefpodoxime 200 mg twice daily are appropriate alternatives. In patients with severe, immediate penicillin hypersensitivity (anaphylaxis, angioedema, or bronchospasm), beta-lactams must be avoided entirely; oral Doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days or a respiratory fluoroquinolone such as Levofloxacin 500 mg once daily or Moxifloxacin 400 mg once daily should be prescribed.

### Section 5: Monitoring, Escalation, and Red-Flag Symptoms
Patients should be instructed to seek immediate emergency medical attention if red-flag symptoms occur: worsening dyspnea, inability to maintain oxygen saturation ≥ 92% on room air, hemoptysis, persistent chest pain, cyanosis, or inability to tolerate oral medications. If a patient fails to show clinical defervescence after 72 hours of appropriate antimicrobial therapy, chest radiography, complete blood count with inflammatory markers, and evaluation for pleural effusion or empyema must be promptly performed.
