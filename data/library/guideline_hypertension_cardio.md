# Clinical Practice Guideline: Adult Hypertension Management & Pharmacological Safety
**Source Title**: WHO & National Cardiology Society Hypertension Guidelines
**Publication Year**: 2024
**Evidence Level**: Ia
**License**: Open Access / Clinical Demo Material

---

### Section 1: Blood Pressure Classification & Cardiovascular Risk
Hypertension in non-pregnant adults is defined as sustained office systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg on at least two separate clinical occasions. Grade 1 hypertension corresponds to 140-159/90-99 mmHg; Grade 2 hypertension corresponds to systolic BP ≥ 160 mmHg or diastolic BP ≥ 100 mmHg. Hypertensive urgency is severe elevation (> 180/120 mmHg) without acute end-organ damage, whereas hypertensive emergency involves acute end-organ damage (encephalopathy, acute coronary syndrome, aortic dissection, acute renal failure, or pulmonary edema) requiring immediate parenteral therapy and ICU admission.

### Section 2: First-Line Antihypertensive Drug Classes
Four primary classes of antihypertensives are recommended for initial therapy:
1. Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEi) such as Enalapril 5 mg to 20 mg once daily or Ramipril 2.5 mg to 10 mg once daily.
2. Angiotensin receptor blockers (ARB) such as Losartan 50 mg to 100 mg once daily or Telmisartan 40 mg to 80 mg once daily.
3. Dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (CCB) such as Amlodipine 5 mg to 10 mg once daily.
4. Thiazide-like diuretics such as Hydrochlorothiazide 12.5 mg to 25 mg once daily or Indapamide 1.5 mg once daily.
For patients with baseline blood pressure ≥ 20/10 mmHg above goal, initial combination therapy with two first-line agents of complementary mechanisms is recommended.

### Section 3: Absolute Contraindications & Patient-Specific Safety Rules
- ACE inhibitors and ARBs are strictly contraindicated during pregnancy (Pregnancy Category D / teratogenic risk: fetal renal dysgenesis, oligohydramnios, skull hypoplasia, and neonatal death) and in bilateral renal artery stenosis or history of angioedema.
- Beta-blockers (such as Atenolol or Metoprolol) should be avoided as first-line monotherapy in uncomplicated hypertension without compelling cardiac indications (e.g. post-myocardial infarction or heart failure with reduced ejection fraction). Non-selective beta-blockers (such as Propranolol) are strictly contraindicated in patients with bronchial asthma due to life-threatening bronchospasm risk.
- In patients with chronic kidney disease (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73m²), potassium-sparing diuretics (e.g. Spironolactone) pose an acute risk of life-threatening hyperkalemia, requiring regular serum potassium and creatinine monitoring within 1 to 2 weeks of initiation.
